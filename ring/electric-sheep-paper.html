Dalarwen: Attack of the Electric Sheep — A Philosophical Study of Thermodynamics, Personal Identity, Network Expansion and Digital Surveillance in a Flooded Welsh Valley

Dr. Melonsis Borealis

Department of Applied Thermodynamics & Personal Identity, University of the Void · in collaboration with the ISAMSJ™ Institute for Absent Signal
Received: 20 years ago · Accepted: 20 years have gone past · Peer-reviewed by: the Cube

Abstract

Dalarwen: Attack of the Electric Sheep is a speculative survival game exploring technological expansion, environmental collapse, personal identity and digital dependency. Set in a remote Welsh valley, it combines dystopian science fiction with cooperative strategy. Its central irony lies in using a networked computer game to criticise universal connectivity, surveillance and society's dependence upon digital infrastructure.

Keywords: thermodynamics · Cartesian dualism · the Allegory of the Cave · electric sheep · renewable instability · Belgium

1. The System: The Non-Static Caravan Park™

The system is powered by the Non-Static Caravan Park, operated by ISAMSJ™. Here, all caravan wheels remain permanently unlocked, while handbrakes are available only through the Premium Survival Package. During Storm Watching Weekends, winds of up to 80 mph transfer kinetic energy into the caravans, producing continuous movement across unstable clifftop pitches. The Infinity Pitch™, which has no meaningful boundary between the pitch and the sea, generates especially high levels of renewable instability.

2. Thermodynamics

This mechanism follows the first law of thermodynamics: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or transformed. Wind, gravity, cliff erosion, laser barbecues and spontaneous caravan descents generate energy that is transmitted through fifteen 5G towers. The towers transform this energy into electric rain and animate the electric sheep.

3. The Electric Sheep and Cartesian Identity

When an electric sheep touches a player, that person splits into an original self and a hostile zombie double. This introduces a Cartesian problem of identity. René Descartes distinguished between the thinking mind and the physical body, famously asserting, “I think, therefore I am.” The zombie double possesses the player's body and appearance but apparently lacks the reflective mind associated with authentic personhood. However, as the zombies begin coordinating their attacks, players must question whether the doubles are genuinely mindless or simply operating under a different form of consciousness. The game therefore asks whether identity resides in the body, in thought or in continuity of memory.

4. Network Expansion and Surveillance

Each zombie increases demand upon the network, intensifying the electric rain and flooding the valley. Players must first connect the towers, using territorial mechanics resembling Ingress. Once all fifteen are connected, universal surveillance becomes possible. Complete connectivity strengthens the sheep, accelerates zombie replication and draws increasing amounts of unstable energy from the caravan park.

5. Shadows: Plato's Cave

This structure also recalls Plato's Allegory of the Cave. Plato describes prisoners who mistake shadows projected upon a wall for reality. In Dalarwen, screens, maps and network data similarly mediate the players' understanding of the valley. The connected network promises knowledge, yet may show only technologically constructed shadows. The wormhole offers a possible escape from this digital cave, although liberation may lead somewhere deeply inconvenient, probably Belgium.

6. The Last Towa

To save Dalarwen, players must dismantle the infrastructure they created. Oat milk can distract sheep, a spacesuit permits travel through electric rain, and the sacred firepit can temporarily restore zombie doubles. In the final level, The Last Towa, Lewis receives five bars of signal just as the valley approaches total submersion. He must choose between destroying the tower and saving Dalarwen or waiting for his thirteenth video to upload.

Conclusion

The game ultimately asks whether connectivity produces knowledge or merely more convincing shadows. Freedom requires the destruction of the network — but also the loss of the signal upon which everyone has become dependent. ISAMSJ™ accepts no philosophical or legal responsibility.
